An RL agent that designs analog equalizers on real silicon — and hits new specs in a handful of simulations.

Give it a target and it sizes a PCIe-Gen2 CTLE (continuous-time linear equalizer) on the open SkyWater 130 nm process, talking to a SPICE simulator in a closed loop, with zero human tuning. Because it learns a design strategy rather than one answer, it retargets to specs it has never seen almost for free.

The problem

Sizing analog circuits is a slow, expert, one-spec-at-a-time loop.

A designer nudges a transistor width, re-runs SPICE, reads the plots, and repeats — hundreds of times, per spec. The parameter space (every W/L, R, C) is combinatorial, so brute-force sweeps are hopeless. SILQ replaces the human in that loop with a reinforcement-learning policy: the environment is a SPICE testbench of the equalizer, the action is the device sizes, the observation is the measured performance-vs-target gap, and the reward is how close the design is to spec.

Headline result

One trained policy hits specs it never saw — far faster than search.

The agent was trained across randomized target-boost specs, then asked to design for eight held-out targets. For each, we count the SPICE simulations needed to reach a passing design, and compare against Bayesian optimization (Optuna TPE) run from scratch per target — because a search has no memory, it pays a full search every time.

SILQ (trained policy) Bayesian search (per spec)
Lower is better. At 11 dB, Bayesian did not converge within 120 simulations (marked DNF). SILQ solved all eight; Bayesian solved seven.

The RL cost is paid once, in training. After that every new spec is nearly free — which is exactly the problem statement's ask: reach near-optimal solutions with fewer search and zero human intervention.

Robustness

The designed equalizer holds across the full PVT grid.

A naïve nominal-only design drifts out of band at 125 °C — the classic PVT trap. An SILQ design, signed off across all 45 corners (process × voltage × temperature) with real HD3 and noise at every corner, meets every hard spec.

How the peaking works

The Rs·Cs across the differential-pair sources creates a zero that boosts the high-frequency (Nyquist) band and flattens the channel loss. The agent trades W/L, tail current, Rs, Cs and the load to place that zero in the 1.25–2.5 GHz window while holding linearity, noise, power and area — the sizing problem, learned.

Why it's fast enough to be real

A resident simulator turns hours of training into minutes.

Loading the SKY130 corner models costs ~15 seconds every time ngspice launches, but a simulation itself is ~40 ms. SILQ keeps the simulator resident and re-evaluates each candidate with alterparam; reset — so models parse once and every evaluation is milliseconds. That measured 350× speedup is the difference between "RL on real SPICE is a nice idea" and "we trained it this afternoon."

  • Real, not behavioral. Every number is measured on SKY130 BSIM transistors in ngspice — AC peaking, HD3 by transient + FFT, input-referred noise by .noise.
  • Generalizing, not memorizing. The policy conditions on the target, so it solves specs held out of training.
  • Honest baselines. A first, naïve RL setup lost to Bayesian search; we reformulated to the sequential design-closing MDP where RL wins — and kept the losing result in the git history.
